## Deal & structure
- Button game with a small and a big blind (WSOP: "A button and blind structure will be used"). Limit is by far the most common structure in rooms; pot-limit, half-pot-limit and no-limit exist but are rarer (Wikipedia, Pagat, Card Player Lifestyle).
- Each player gets **four** cards face down. No community cards.
- Betting round 1 (pre-draw) starts left of the big blind. Players may call, raise or fold; the big blind can raise even if everyone just calls (Pagat).
- Three draws, each followed by a betting round. In every draw each player, starting from the first active player left of the button, may replace 0 (stand pat) to 4 cards. So four betting rounds total: pre-draw, after draw 1, after draw 2, after draw 3. Post-draw betting starts with the first active player left of the button (small blind or next live seat).
- Limit betting: small bet on the first two rounds, big bet (double) on the last two (1-1-2-2) - Pagat, BARGE, Wikipedia.
- Dealer procedure at the WSOP: draw cards are "pushed and pulled" to the player; if a player lets the dealer pitch them, any card that comes up exposed stays with that player (WSOP #302). A player may receive four consecutive cards off the deck (#300).
- If the deck runs out during a draw, discards are reshuffled (Wikipedia, Pagat). WSOP's lowball/Archie procedure: shuffle prior discards, mucks and burns; do not use cards discarded in the current draw round; use the very last stub card only if it completes all draws.
- Entering: a new player must wait for the big blind or post a full big blind (WSOP #301).

## Showdown / what wins
- The pot goes to the best **badugi**: the lowest set of cards with no two sharing a rank and no two sharing a suit. Ace is low. Straights and flushes mean nothing as such (they just reduce your card count).
- Reduce your four cards to the largest subset with all different ranks and all different suits. If duplicates exist you choose which to drop so the remaining hand is best.
  - 4 cards (a true badugi) beats any 3-card hand, which beats any 2-card hand, which beats any 1-card hand. Even K-Q-J-10 rainbow beats A-2-3 plus a duplicate (WSOP #298, Pagat, BARGE).
  - Within the same number of cards, compare the highest card first (lower wins), then the next highest, etc. So A-2-3-4 rainbow (the "wheel") is the nuts; A-2-3-5, A-2-4-5, A-3-4-5, 2-3-4-5, A-2-3-6... follow (PokerNews table).
  - WSOP example: Ad-2c-3h-6h vs Ac-3d-4h-5h. Each plays as a 3-card hand because two hearts collide: A-2-3 beats A-3-4 (WSOP #298).
  - Unused cards never break ties: two hands that reduce to the same ranks split. BARGE example: 7s-3s-2h-Ad and 3c-3d-2s-Ah both play 3-2-A and chop. Another BARGE example: 7-4-2-2 (plays 7-4-2) beats 7-5-A-A (plays 7-5-A).
  - There is no suit ranking; equal ranks tie and split (Pagat).
- Players show all four cards at showdown even if only 1-3 count; cards speak (Pagat).
- Ties split the pot evenly; odd chip is broken down to the smallest chip used for the blinds (WSOP #152). Badugi has no dedicated odd-chip rule, so the general WSOP button-game rule applies: first tied hand clockwise from the button gets it (WSOP #153), except the WSOP "Mixed Games" simplification which gives it to the worst position in all button games. ## Rooms disagree
- Number of draws / structure: WSOP "when played limit stakes, this game will usually be played with three draws"; Pagat/BARGE/Wikipedia all agree on 3 draws, 1-1-2-2 limit. PokerNews adds that it is "usually fixed limit". No source I read gave a non-3-draw standard.
- Max players: WSOP 8, Pagat 2-8, Wikipedia says "usually six-handed", Card Player Lifestyle says triple-draw games are usually kept to six in rooms.
- Origin story: Pagat attributes early play to Winnipeg ("Off Suit Lowball", 1980s), Korea (1980s) and Southern US prisons, and says Paul "Eskimo" Clark popularized it in the US around 2004; Wikipedia says several sources credit Paul Clark with bringing it from Vietnam. These conflict; treat the history as unsettled.
- WSOP event history: Wikipedia and PokerNews both say the WSOP added a $1,500 Badugi tournament in 2023. PokerNews' own year/event table rows are internally inconsistent, so quote only "since 2023" and cite Wikipedia/PokerNews for it.

## Common newcomer mistakes
- Treating a paired or suited hand as still a "four-card" hand. Any repeated suit or rank cuts you down to 3, 2 or 1 cards.
- Thinking a low 3-card hand (A-2-3 + dup) beats a high true badugi (K-Q-J-10). The number of cards comes first.
- Forgetting that you choose which duplicate to discard; drop the card that leaves the best combination.
- Overplaying high badugis (K-Q-9-6 on the first draw): if opponents draw one to low 3-card hands, they'll often make something better (PokerNews tip).
- Drawing three or four from early position (George Danzer quote via PokerNews): it screams weakness and you're out of position.
- Not counting outs: a three-card badugi drawing one has 10 outs (cards of the missing suit that don't pair a rank you hold) per Wikipedia; about a 51% chance to hit one by the river over three draws (Wikipedia, PokerNews).
- "Snowing": standing pat with a non-badugi is a legitimate bluff in Badugi (PokerNews, Wikipedia), but it's a strategy note not a rule.

## Lore & reputation
- Name believed to come from a Korean word meaning a black-and-white spotted dog (also the Korean name for Go, "baduk") (Pagat, Wikipedia, PokerNews).
- Pagat notes two earlier North Carolina home-game "doogie" hi-lo games from 2000 that used the same hand idea; the "doogie" name may come from Doogie Howser (Pagat speculates).
- Included in WSOP Dealer's Choice (listed in PokerNews' and PokerListings' 20-game lists), the WSOP Triple Draw Mix, and a stand-alone $1,500 event since 2023 (Wikipedia). Offered online mostly on PokerStars (PokerNews).
- Parent of Badeucy, Badacey, Badugi Hi-Lo, Hi-dugi and the Drawmaha-dugi family (BARGE, Card Player).
